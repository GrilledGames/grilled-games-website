GitHub has been going through a public image crisis recently with respect to their reliability. In April of 2026 GitHub reached zero nines of uptime. If you don't know what "nines" means with respect to uptime it refers to how many times the number nine appears in a service's uptime. Small organizations will aim for two nines (99%) of uptime meaning they are only down about 3.65 / 365 days/year. Large organizations will aim for four nines (99.99%) of uptime meaning they are only down about 52.56 / 525,600 minutes/year. When GitHub reached zero nines of uptime, around 88%, it was estimated they would be down for 43 days out of the year if the situation did not change. This lead to numerous public breakups with GitHub being called "no longer a place for serious work". Then why are we jumping to defend GitHub?

Earlier in August we received our second annual Hostinger website hosting bill for $135.25. This is an amount that most people could look past, but not a low volume business trying to keep their purse strings tight and become profitable. This left us with a 30 day window to research, migrate, and claim our refund.

The solution we landed on was a lesser know GitHub feature, GitHub Pages. GitHub Pages lets you host static websites directly from your repository. This was our solution for two reasons, the cost and the flexibility. GitHub Pages is a freemium feature with the free tier doing everything we need. 1GB of storage, 100GB/month of bandwidth and 10 builds/hour are not limits we will be hitting; if we do then we have a good issue on our hands. Additionally, since the static website is just code, we can make any website we want without being constrained to Hostinger's website builder. Hostinger isn't all bad though because GitHub Pages also lets us use our affordable Hostinger domain, grilledgames.com, over the GitHub provided domain. The free tier does restrict you to hosting from a public repository however. This is something we were able to look past because all the data in the repository is intended for the public anyways. Also our store doesn't phone home to GitHub, it takes you directly to Stripe checkout. That means out customer data is not handled by GitHub at all, 

The experience with the new website stack has been great so far. On the "backend" we are using Jekyll, a static site generator that turns markdown, templates, and structured data into websites. On the CI/CD side Jekyll is already integrated with GitHub Pages. We did not have to set up any actions for GitHub to build and update the website on commit. Something we miss though was the simplified store management on Hostinger. Most of our tax information carried over from the Hostinger side since it also uses Stripe but setting up payment links was much more involved than we anticipated. Since the responsibility of collecting sales tax moved from Hostinger to us directly there was a lot of follow up research on when do you become responsible for collecting sales tax? What is a sales tax nexus? What are the conditions for being "engaged in business"? It turns out due to our volume and physical presence we are only responsible for collecting sales tax in two states.

Ultimately technology choices are very personal. For us we would rather minimize costs and get our hands dirty over maximizing platform features and reliability. This win and GitHub's uptime improvements have led us to this successful website migration and putting a pause on researching ways to migrate private repositories for our main projects, games.
